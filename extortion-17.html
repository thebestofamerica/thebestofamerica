Extortion 17

U.S. Navy  ·  U.S. Army  ·  U.S. Air Force  ·  Tangi Valley, Afghanistan  ·  August 6, 2011

They lifted off into the dark to save their brothers on the ground below. Thirty Americans, seven Afghan commandos, an interpreter, and a combat assault dog named Bart never made it back.

01
The
Mission

A platoon of Rangers went in after a Taliban commander. The fight didn't go as planned.

On the night of August 5, 2011, a platoon of 47 U.S. Army Rangers from the 75th Ranger Regiment left a forward operating base in Logar Province, carried by two CH-47D Chinook helicopters, to capture or kill Qari Tahir, a senior Taliban commander operating out of the Tangi Valley in Wardak Province.

The raid met fierce resistance. As the firefight stretched on and enemy fighters massed against the Rangers on the ground, commanders made the call every special operations unit trains and waits for: send in reinforcements.

02
Extortion 17
Answers

Twenty-two Navy personnel, five Army aircrew, three airmen, and seven Afghan commandos boarded one Chinook.

A Quick Reaction Force scrambled to reinforce the Rangers — 22 Navy personnel, including 17 SEALs from Naval Special Warfare Development Group's Gold Squadron, five Army National Guard and Reserve aircrew, three Air Force personnel, seven Afghan National Army commandos, and an Afghan interpreter. They boarded a CH-47D Chinook carrying the callsign "Extortion 17" and lifted off into the dark toward the fight below.

Among them was Bart, a combat assault dog assigned to Naval Special Warfare Development Group, and his handler, Petty Officer 1st Class John Douangdara.

★
03
The
Shootdown

As the Chinook slowed to land, a single rocket-propelled grenade found it.

As Extortion 17 closed on the landing zone at roughly 100 to 150 feet, a Taliban fighter fired a rocket-propelled grenade into the aircraft. The Chinook went down instantly, killing everyone aboard.

A U.S. Army CH-47D Chinook, the same model as Extortion 17, during operations in Afghanistan.
Representative photo — not the incident aircraft.

Investigators concluded the RPG struck the Chinook's aft rotor system as the aircraft slowed on approach, causing an immediate and unrecoverable loss of control — the single deadliest incident for American forces in the entire Afghanistan War, and the greatest loss of life in the history of U.S. Naval Special Warfare.

Summary of findings — U.S. Central Command investigation, 2011
★
04
Bart &
His Handler

Handler and dog trained together, deployed together, and were lost together.

His handler, Petty Officer 1st Class John Douangdara, 26, of South Sioux City, Nebraska, had enlisted in the Navy as a teenager and risen to become the SEALs' lead dog handler. Handler and dog trained together, deployed together, and were lost together aboard Extortion 17.

Douangdara and Bart, Afghanistan
Photo: Military Working Dog Team Support Association (MWDTSA)

Retired Navy SEAL officer Mike Sarraille has recounted that when the RPG struck the Chinook's rotor and the aircraft went into auto-rotation, Douangdara's first instinct was to reach for Bart and shield him — the same protective bond, Sarraille has said, that exists between any two brothers-in-arms. Sarraille has further recounted that the force of the crash was severe enough that recovery teams found the two fused together, a process that took roughly two hours to separate.

His first reaction was to grab his dog, Bart... and protect him, best he possibly could.

— Mike Sarraille, retired U.S. Navy SEAL officer, recounting John Douangdara's final act

Source note: this account comes from Mike Sarraille's own recollection — recounted on Fox News in connection with the Fox Nation documentary Final Flight: The Fall of Extortion 17, and in greater detail in the "Gold Shields" podcast interview Navy SEAL Tier One Operator: Combat Missions & Extortion 17 (Feb. 2026) — not an official DoD casualty report. It is included here with direct attribution to him, as the archive's standard for verified fact.

Theirs was one bond among many that night. Every man aboard Extortion 17 answered the same call, carried the same risk, and gave the same last full measure.

★
05
What It
Cost

Sons, brothers, husbands, and fathers — thirty Americans who never hesitated to answer the call.

Thirty Americans, seven Afghan commandos, an interpreter, and a working dog gave everything answering a call for help that night. More than 25 children lost a father. Fifteen years on, Extortion 17 remains a solemn reminder of what reinforcement, in its truest sense, demands — and of a loyalty, both human and canine, that never once hesitated to answer it.

Sons, brothers, husbands, and — more importantly — fathers.

— Mike Sarraille, retired U.S. Navy SEAL officer, on the men of Extortion 17

The Best of America™ archive records their names here because an archive that tells the story of this nation must include the men — and the dog — who gave the last full measure answering a call for help in a valley most Americans will never find on a map.
