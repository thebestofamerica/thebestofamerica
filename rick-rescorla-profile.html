Rick Rescorla

Colonel, U.S. Army (Ret.)  ·  Vice President of Security, Morgan Stanley  ·  South Tower, World Trade Center

He had spent four years insisting the towers would be attacked again, drilling employees who thought he was paranoid. When it happened, he ignored the announcement telling everyone to stay at their desks, grabbed a bullhorn, and started singing to keep 2,700 people calm on the way down — then went back up for the ones still left.

01
A Soldier
Twice Over

He fought for two countries before he ever set foot in the towers he would spend years trying to protect.

Cyril Richard Rescorla was born in Hayle, a small town in Cornwall, England, in 1939, and grew up on stories of American GIs who had passed through during World War II. He joined the British Army as a teenager, served with the Parachute Regiment during the Cyprus Emergency, then worked as a police officer in Rhodesia and London before immigrating to the United States in 1963 to enlist in the U.S. Army.

He fought in Vietnam as a platoon leader with the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry — the same unit made famous in the Battle of Ia Drang, the first major engagement between American and North Vietnamese forces. Rescorla was decorated for valor and became known for an unusual habit under fire: he sang to his men, old British and Cornish songs, to steady their nerves in the middle of combat. He became a U.S. citizen in 1967. By the time he retired from the Army as a colonel and went to work in corporate security for Morgan Stanley at the World Trade Center, singing under pressure was not a trick. It was simply who he was.

02
The Man
Who Would
Not Relax

He said the towers would be hit again. He said it for years. People rolled their eyes, and he made them drill anyway.

Rescorla had already lived through one attack on the World Trade Center: the 1993 bombing, when he personally led Morgan Stanley's evacuation from the South Tower. Rather than treat it as a one-time scare, he became convinced the buildings remained a target — and grew specific about how the next attack might come, including the possibility of an aircraft. He recommended Morgan Stanley relocate its offices to New Jersey. The firm, the largest tenant in the South Tower, wasn't going to move. So he did the next best thing: he trained the people who couldn't leave.

For years, Rescorla ran mandatory fire drills that employees grumbled about — timed evacuations down twenty flights of stairs, two by two, no elevators, no exceptions, regardless of how it disrupted the trading floor. Executives complained about the cost in lost productivity. He held the drills anyway. It is the same discipline that shows up in anyone who has decided, quietly and without asking permission, to prepare for a day they hope never comes.

03
“Keep
Moving”

The loudspeaker told everyone to stay at their desks. He ignored it and started singing.

On the morning of September 11, 2001, Rescorla was on the 44th floor of the South Tower when the North Tower was struck. A Port Authority announcement told occupants of the South Tower to remain at their desks — the building was safe, it said. Rescorla, who had spent four years preparing for exactly this moment, did not wait for permission. He grabbed a bullhorn and a walkie-talkie and began ordering all twenty floors of Morgan Stanley employees down the stairwells, two by two, exactly as they had drilled.

Then the second plane hit the South Tower itself, just above the floors he was clearing. The building shook. Panic started to spread in the stairwells. Rescorla's voice cut through it — first with instructions, then with song, an old Welsh battle hymn he had sung to soldiers in Vietnam decades earlier. Employees who lived to tell the story remember filing down twenty flights of stairs listening to a sixty-two-year-old security chief sing to them like it was any other drill.

Remain calm. Help each other. Be proud to be Americans. We will get through this.

— Rick Rescorla, over the bullhorn, South Tower stairwell, September 11, 2001
★
04
One More
Person

He wasn't even supposed to be in the building that day. He'd traded his day off so a colleague could have it instead.

By the time nearly all of Morgan Stanley's 2,700 employees were out, colleagues told Rescorla he had done what he needed to do — it was time to leave. He pointed back up the stairwell. Somewhere above them, people were still screaming. He was last seen on the tenth floor, heading up, looking for anyone left behind. The South Tower collapsed at 9:59 a.m. He did not come out.

During the evacuation, Rescorla managed to call his wife, Susan. It was, by every account she has given since, a goodbye. He told her not to cry, that he needed to finish getting his people out safely, and then he said the words she has carried ever since.

If something happens to me, I want you to know I've never been happier.

— Rick Rescorla, final phone call to his wife Susan, September 11, 2001

05
Why He
Belongs Here

He spent four years being the person nobody wanted to listen to. That is exactly why 2,700 people got to go home.

Rescorla's body was never recovered. His name is inscribed at the National September 11 Memorial, where a white rose is placed beside it every year on his birthday, May 27. A train in his native Cornwall now carries his name. But the truest monument to Rick Rescorla is a number: roughly 2,700 people who walked out of the South Tower alive because a security chief refused to stop insisting, year after year, drill after unpopular drill, that the worst could happen and that being ready mattered more than being liked.

This archive exists for the moments before the emergency, not just the moment of it. Rescorla's real courage wasn't only the bullhorn and the singing on September 11 — it was the years of being the guy who wouldn't let people get comfortable, who kept running drills everyone else thought were a waste of time. Anyone preparing for a hard season they can feel coming — a diagnosis, a layoff, a fight they know is on the way — can take something real from that: the discipline that looks unnecessary right up until the day it saves everyone in the building.
